What matters most to you?

Screening involves tradeoffs, and people weigh them differently. Move each slider to where you sit, then take the summary to your doctor.

This tool does not recommend a screening plan and does not give medical advice. It helps you put words to what matters to you so you can raise it with your doctor, who knows your risk.

  1. False alarms vs. missed cancer

    A false alarm worries me most Missing something worries me most

    Being called back for a scare is what you dread most. Tell your doctor plainly, and ask exactly what the follow-up process involves and how fast you would get an answer.

    A callback would weigh on you heavily. Ask how often follow-up testing turns out to be benign, and what support is available while you wait.

    You would feel a false alarm more than most people. Ask what happens between an abnormal result and a final answer.

    You lean slightly toward worrying about false alarms. Ask your doctor how often a callback leads to nothing.

    You weigh both about equally. Ask your doctor to walk you through what a callback would mean for you, alongside what a missed finding would mean.

    You lean slightly toward worrying about a missed finding. Ask whether anything in your history makes a missed finding more likely.

    You would accept some extra callbacks to lower the chance of missing something. Ask which tests are most sensitive at your risk level.

    You would put up with a good deal of follow-up to catch something early. Ask what additional screening would involve for you.

    Not missing anything matters most to you, even with many callbacks. Tell your doctor you would rather investigate than wait, and ask what that looks like in practice.

  2. Radiation exposure

    Radiation worries me It's a non-issue for me

    Radiation is a serious concern for you. Raise it first, and ask about breast ultrasound and breast MRI, which use no radiation at all.

    Radiation genuinely worries you. Ask your doctor whether any part of your screening plan could use a test without radiation.

    You would rather limit radiation where you reasonably can. Ask how many mammograms your plan would involve over the next ten years.

    Radiation is a mild concern for you. Ask what your cumulative exposure would look like.

    You are neither especially worried nor dismissive. Ask what your total exposure would look like on the schedule you are considering.

    Radiation is a small factor for you. You may want to spend the conversation on which test is most accurate for you.

    Radiation is a minor consideration. Focus on which test finds cancer most reliably at your risk level.

    Radiation barely registers for you. Saying so lets the conversation go elsewhere.

    Radiation is not something you weigh at all. That frees the conversation to focus on what matters more to you.

  3. Waiting for results

    Waiting is the hardest part I'd rather wait longer for a thorough read

    The waiting is the worst part for you. Ask who will call you, exactly when, and whether results can come any sooner.

    Waiting is very hard for you. Ask for the typical turnaround where you would be treated, and how you will be told.

    Waiting is stressful for you. Ask what the timeline usually looks like so there are no surprises.

    You would rather not wait long. Ask when you should expect to hear, and who to call if you do not.

    You can tolerate the wait as long as you know what to expect. Ask for a realistic timeline up front.

    You can sit with a wait if it means a careful read. Ask what the reading process involves.

    You would rather the reading be unhurried. Ask whether a second read is standard, and what that adds to the timeline.

    You would happily wait longer for a more careful review. Ask what a thorough read involves and who performs it.

    A careful read matters far more to you than a fast one. Tell your doctor you would rather wait than have anything rushed.

  4. Screening frequency

    Screen me more often, even with more callbacks Space it out, even if something's caught a bit later

    You want screening as often as your risk level allows. Ask what the most frequent appropriate schedule would be for you.

    You lean strongly toward frequent screening. Ask whether yearly screening is recommended at your risk level.

    You would prefer the shorter interval. Ask what your risk category suggests.

    You lean slightly toward screening more often. Ask what the difference between the intervals means for you.

    You are open to either interval. Ask your doctor which one your risk level points to, and why.

    You lean slightly toward spacing screening out. Ask what the longer interval would mean at your risk level.

    You would prefer the longer interval if it is reasonable for you. Ask what spacing is safe given your risk.

    You would rather screen less often. Ask your doctor what the longest safe interval is for you.

    You want screening spaced out as much as is safe. Ask what the longest safe interval is at your risk level, and what the tradeoff is.

  5. Cost and access

    I want the most thorough option regardless of cost Cost and coverage matter a lot to my choice

    Cost is not a constraint for you. Spend the conversation on which test is most accurate at your risk level.

    You would pay for the most thorough option. It is still worth confirming what is covered so nothing surprises you.

    You lean toward thoroughness over cost. Ask what the out-of-pocket difference would actually be.

    Cost is a minor factor for you. Ask for an estimate before you schedule.

    You are weighing thoroughness against cost. Ask for an out-of-pocket estimate for each option you are considering.

    Cost matters to you. Ask which of the options are covered by your plan.

    Cost is a real factor. Ask which facilities are in network and whether you need a referral.

    Cost will strongly shape what you choose. Call your insurance before scheduling to confirm coverage.

    Cost and coverage will decide this for you. Call your insurance first and ask what is covered, which facilities are in network, and whether a referral is needed.
