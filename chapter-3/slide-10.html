What matters most to you?

Screening involves tradeoffs, and people weigh them differently. Move each slider to where you sit, then take the summary to your doctor.

This tool does not recommend a screening plan and does not give medical advice. It helps you put words to what matters to you so you can raise it with your doctor, who knows your risk.

  1. False alarms vs. missed cancer

    A false alarm worries me most Missing something worries me most

    Being called back for a scare weighs on you more than anything else. Tell your doctor that, and ask what the follow-up process actually involves and how long it takes to get an answer.

    You would feel a callback more than most. Ask your doctor how often follow-up testing turns out to be benign, and what support is available while you wait.

    You weigh both about equally. Ask your doctor to walk you through what a callback would mean for you, alongside what a missed finding would mean.

    You would put up with some false alarms to lower the chance of missing something. Ask your doctor which tests are most sensitive for someone at your risk level.

    Not missing anything matters most to you, even if that means more callbacks. Tell your doctor you would rather investigate a finding than leave it, and ask what that would look like in practice.

  2. Radiation exposure

    Radiation worries me It's a non-issue for me

    Radiation is a real concern for you. Bring it up directly, and note that breast ultrasound and breast MRI use no radiation at all, which is worth asking about for your situation.

    You would like to keep radiation exposure down. Ask your doctor how many mammograms your screening plan would involve over the next ten years.

    You are neither especially worried nor dismissive. Ask what your total exposure would look like on the schedule you are considering.

    Radiation is a minor consideration for you. You may want to focus the conversation on which test finds cancer most reliably at your risk level.

    Radiation is not something you weigh. Saying so frees the conversation to focus on what matters more to you.

  3. Waiting for results

    Waiting is the hardest part I'd rather wait longer for a thorough read

    The wait itself is the hard part for you. Ask your doctor who will call you, when, and whether there is any way to get results sooner.

    Waiting is stressful for you. Ask what the typical turnaround is where you would be treated, and how you will be told.

    You can tolerate the wait as long as you know what to expect. Ask for a realistic timeline up front.

    You would rather the reading be unhurried. Ask whether a second read is standard, and what that adds to the timeline.

    A careful read matters more to you than a fast one. Tell your doctor you would rather wait than be rushed, and ask what a thorough review involves.

  4. Screening frequency

    Screen me more often, even with more callbacks Space it out, even if something's caught a bit later

    You lean toward screening as often as your risk level allows. Ask your doctor what the most frequent schedule appropriate for you would be.

    You would prefer the shorter interval. Ask what your risk category suggests, and whether yearly screening is recommended for you.

    You are open to either interval. Ask your doctor which one your risk level points to, and why.

    You would prefer the longer interval if it is reasonable for you. Ask what spacing is safe given your risk.

    You would rather screen less often. Ask your doctor what the longest safe interval is for someone at your risk level, and what the tradeoff is.

  5. Cost and access

    I want the most thorough option regardless of cost Cost and coverage matter a lot to my choice

    Cost is not a constraint for you. Focus the conversation on which test is most accurate for your risk level.

    You would stretch for the more thorough option. It is still worth asking what is covered so there are no surprises.

    You are weighing thoroughness against cost. Ask for an out-of-pocket estimate for each option you are considering.

    Cost is a real factor for you. Ask which facilities are in network and whether you need a referral.

    Cost and coverage will shape your decision. Call your insurance before scheduling and ask what is covered, which facilities are in network, and whether a referral is needed.
